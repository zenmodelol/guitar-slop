B

Practising without a teacher

Find the control tempo

The tempo where you play something perfectly three times in a row. Practise there. Mistakes repeated at speed get learned as well as the right notes.

Three tempos

Control (perfect), target (the goal in the lesson), stretch (10% above target for 30 seconds). Then drop back. Your control tempo rises over the week.

The 80% rule

Right less than 80% of the time? Make the task smaller: fewer notes, slower, one bar. Right more than 95%? Make it harder.

Say it out loud

Name notes and their jobs as you play ("E, the 3rd"). It feels silly and it is the fastest way to connect theory to your hands.

Record weekly

You cannot hear your own timing while playing. On playback you can. Your recording is your teacher's ears.

Keep a log

Date, exercise, tempo reached, one note on what was hard. Five lines a day. It shows you progress on bad days.

The chord-change toolkit

The most common sticking point at this level. Chord changes are slow for four reasons: you do not know the destination, your fingers lift too far, all fingers move at once, or you practise chords instead of changes. These six drills attack those causes. Every lesson from Week 2 on asks for one of them.

One-minute change timer

Your log is saved

A · One-minute changes

Two chords, 60 seconds, one strum each, count clean changes with the timer above. Three pairs a day. Most people double their count in two weeks.

B · Find the pivot

Before playing a change, look at both chords and find which fingers stay (common tones), which slide along a string (keep contact), and which jump. Move stay and slide fingers first.

C · Ghost change

Hold chord one. Lift a millimetre, form chord two in the air, land all fingers together. Ten slow reps. Your hand learns to arrive with the shape made.

D · Change early on purpose

Strum beats 1, 2, 3, and use beat 4 as the change, strumming open or muted strings while you move. Shrink the gap to the "and" of 4. Working players do this.

E · Top-note melody

Pick voicings whose highest note moves by a step or stays. Hum it. Your ear then leads you to the next chord.

F · Name before you play

Say the destination before landing: "G7, root on the 6th string, 3rd fret". When you know where you're going, the hand follows.

D

Reading the tab and grids

Tab lines are strings, high e on top. Numbers are frets; stacked numbers are played together. Spacing is even: each slot is one rhythmic step (8ths, 16ths or triplets, stated in the exercise header), and a dash means let the previous note ring or rest. Letters after a fret: h hammer-on, p pull-off, t tap, b bend up a whole step, / slide in, x muted. Press Play to hear the rhythm; that is the fastest way to read it.

E

Health and setup

  • Warm up before hard stretches and fast playing. Stop if you feel sharp pain; rest a day if you feel aching in the wrist or forearm.
  • Press only as hard as needed: play a chord, release until it buzzes, add just enough pressure back.
  • Sit or stand with the neck angled up a little; keep the fretting wrist as straight as the shape allows.
  • A clean amp tone shows mistakes. Practise clean; add drive for fun.

The twelve notes

1.5

What a triad is

A triad is three notes: a root, a 3rd and a 5th. C major is C (root), E (3rd), G (5th). The 3rd decides major or minor; the 5th adds weight. Every chord you play, including your 9ths and 13ths, has a triad at its core, so triads are the most efficient chords to learn.

On three adjacent strings, those three notes can be stacked three ways, called inversions:

Root position

Root lowest: R – 3 – 5. The most stable sound.

1st inversion

3rd lowest: 3 – 5 – R. Lighter, often used for the IV or V chord.

2nd inversion

5th lowest: 5 – R – 3. Open, a little unresolved.

Each string set has three shapes per octave. Learn them as jobs, not grips: in every shape, know which string has the root.

2.4

Three rules of voice leading

  1. A note shared by both chords stays put. C and F share C; F and G share nothing; G and C share G.
  2. Every other note moves to the nearest note of the next chord, usually one or two frets on the same string.
  3. The top note is the melody. Listen to the high e string as you change. Make it move smoothly.

These three rules work the same way for 7th chords and extended chords in Part II. Learning them on triads first is why your later chord changes will speed up.

Key signatures without a list

  • Order of sharps: F C G D A E B ("Father Charles Goes Down And Ends Battle"). Order of flats: the same backwards, B E A D G C F.

Voice-leading rules for 7th chords

  1. Keep common tones under the same finger.
  2. Move every other voice to the nearest chord tone, usually by one or two frets.

12.1

Transcription method

  1. Choose something short and loved: 4–8 bars you can already sing.
  2. Sing it until you can sing along exactly.
  3. Find the first note by singing and searching on one string, then each next note by its distance from the last.
  4. Slow it down to 50–75% with an app that keeps pitch (Transcribe!, Amazing Slow Downer, or YouTube's speed control).
  5. Find the position where the phrase sits; players usually stay in one position per phrase.
  6. Write it as tab with rough rhythms underneath.
  7. Analyse: chord tones on strong beats? Scale? Approach notes? Steal one idea and use it in your own solos for a week.

17.1

Swing feel

Jazz 8th notes are not even. Think of each beat as a triplet and play the first and last parts: long–short, "doo-ba doo-ba". At medium tempos the ratio is closer to even than a strict triplet; at slow tempos it is more pronounced. Two more ingredients make it swing:

  • Accent the offbeats slightly (the "ba"), not the downbeats.
  • Ghost some notes, especially the ones on the beat in fast lines, so the line has shape.

Swing drill

Metronome on 2 and 4 · 100 bpm

  2. Switch the metronome to 2 and 4 and keep the same feel.
  3. Accent every offbeat. Then every offbeat except the last of each phrase.
  4. Sing along with a Wes Montgomery or Grant Green record for 2 minutes, matching their feel, before you play.

Motif development

A motif is a short idea. Here is one (A C D over Am7), then four ways to develop it:

  1. Bar 1: the motif.
  2. Bar 2: displaced by an 8th note (same notes, new rhythm position).

How to Tackle a Standard

Twenty-seven songs, tunes and grooves, ordered by difficulty: eight rock songs to start, then jazz, soul and funk, each with its changes, an analysis, and step-by-step guides for comping and soloing. This page is the method that every tune page follows.

What is here and what is notEach page gives the chord changes and how to play them. A

Reading the charts

  • Each box is one bar. Two chords in a box share the bar (two beats each). % repeats the previous bar.
  • Under each chord name is its Roman numeral in the tune's key. Lower case = minor. ♭VI means the chord built on the lowered 6th degree.
  • Black tabs (A, B, C) mark the sections of the form.
  • Key transposes everything. Play the changes plays bass and chords so you can hear the harmony and practise along.
  • Show voicings generates a voice-led voicing for every chord, in the type and neck zone you choose: triads, shells or drop-2s. Each is chosen so that the notes move as little as possible from the previous chord. Use them as a starting point, then find your own.
B

Learning a tune: the order

  1. Listen to at least two recordings. Pick one as your model for tempo and feel.
  2. Sing the melody until you can sing it without the record.
  3. Learn the form: how many bars, where sections begin, where the ii–Vs and key changes are. Each tune page's "What's going on" section does this with you.
  4. Learn the melody by ear in one position, then another.
  5. Comp through the five comping stages below.
  6. Solo through the six soloing stages below.
  7. Memorise it. A tune you need a chart for is a tune you are reading, not playing.
C

The five comping stages

Stage 1

Roots

Play the root of every chord on beat 1 with the playback. You learn the form and the bass line. Say chord names out loud.

Stage 2

Triads

Voicings set to triads, strings 1-2-3 or 2-3-4. One zone, whole tune, no stops. This is the harmony at its simplest.

Stage 3

Shells

Root, 3rd, 7th. Now you hear every chord's real quality. Notice the guide tones moving by step.

Stage 4

Drop-2

Four-note voicings in one zone, then another zone. Watch the top note: make it a melody.

Stage 5

Rhythm and colour

Replace whole-note strums with the tune's comping rhythm (each page suggests one). Add 9ths and 13ths on dominant chords. Leave space.

Stage test

Can you…

Comp the whole form from memory at the target tempo, in two zones, while counting the bars out loud? Then move on.

D

The six soloing stages

Stage 1

The melody

Play it, then play it with small changes: rhythm, an added note, an octave shift. The melody is the best first solo.

Stage 2

Roots and 3rds

One note per chord, on beat 1: the root, then the 3rd. You hear the changes clearly.

Stage 3

Arpeggios

Arpeggiate each chord in one position, starting on the 3rd. Then start from the note nearest your last note.

Stage 4

Guide-tone line

A slow line of 3rds and 7ths moving by step. Each page gives one. Then decorate it with approach notes and enclosures.

Stage 5

Scale colours

Each page gives a scale map. Use it to fill between chord tones, landing on a chord tone at each change.

Stage 6

Phrases and motifs

Short ideas, repeated and varied, with space. Quote the melody. Steal from your transcriptions.

FeelMedium swing

Twelve bars that move from minor to major and through four keys. Short, dense and a superb ii–V workout.

The changes

What's going on

  • Bars 1–5: Cm(maj7), then Gm7 – C7 – Fmaj7: a ii–V into F major.
  • Bars 7–9: Fm7 – B♭7 – E♭maj7: the same move a whole step down.
  • Bars 10–11: E♭m7 – A♭7 – D♭maj7: and again, a whole step down.
  • Bar 12: Dm7♭5 – G7♭9: minor ii–V back to Cm.

The tune is the same idea (a ii–V–I) stepping down by whole steps. Learn one comping pattern for it and move it.

Comping

  1. Cm(maj7): a minor triad with a major 7th; drop-2 shape from the finder (Chord: m(maj7)).
  2. Use the same drop-2 inversion for each ii–V–I and slide it down two frets.

The progression is so widely used in R&B, neo-soul and hip-hop that it is often just called by its numbers: the 4–3–6. Learn it in every key.

The changes

What's going on

In F minor: D♭maj7 (♭VI) – C7 (V) – Fm7 (i) – E♭m7 A♭7 (a ii–V into D♭, which starts the loop again). Thought of in A♭ major, it is IVmaj7 – III7 – vi – v – I7, the version in Week 23.

Comping

  1. maj9 on D♭, 7♯9 or 13 on C7, m9 on Fm7 and E♭m7, 13 on A♭7.
  2. Play behind the beat (Week 23), with slides into chords and short fills on the last beat of each 4-bar loop.

Offline & Backup

Run Fretwork on your iPad without an internet connection, and keep your progress when you move between devices.

A

Put it on your iPad

The course comes as an offline package (fretwork-offline.zip): the page, an app manifest, icons and a small offline script. Once it is hosted at any web address and opened in Safari, you can add it to your Home Screen, and it then works without a connection.

  1. Host the folder once. Unzip the package on a computer and upload the folder to a free static host: Netlify Drop (drag the folder onto the page; sign in so the site is kept), GitHub Pages, or Cloudflare Pages. Any host that serves plain files works.
  2. Open the address in Safari on the iPad. Browse a few chapters so everything loads.
  3. Share → Add to Home Screen. Fretwork now opens full screen like an app.
  4. Open it once while online after adding it; from then on it works offline.
Opening the file directlyTapping the HTML file in the iPad Files app shows a preview that may not run the interactive tools. Host it as above, or open the single-file version in a desktop browser.

Other ways

  • Desktop: double-click fretwork.html; it runs in any modern browser, no install needed.
  • Home network: on a computer, run python3 -m http.server in the unzipped folder, then open http://<computer's IP>:8000 in Safari on the iPad (same Wi-Fi; not offline).

Sound on iPad

Audio starts after your first tap. If you hear nothing, check the volume and that the iPad is not in silent mode.

B

Back up and move your progress

Ticks, quiz bests, change-timer logs and your routine length are stored in the browser for the address you use. A new address (for example moving from this hosted page to your iPad copy) starts empty. Create a backup here, then restore it on the other copy.
